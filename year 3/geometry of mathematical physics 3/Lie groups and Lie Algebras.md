
# Motivating examples:

## $U(1)$:
The group $U(1)$ derives its name from unitary complex $1\times1$ matrices. Acting with $g$ on a complex number $c$ we define a map:$$\Huge z\rightarrow gz,\,\,g\in U(1),\,\,z\in\mathbb{C}$$where we require that the inner form $|z|^2$ remains unchanged as:$$\Huge |z|^2=\bar zz\rightarrow\bar z\bar ggz=|g|^2|z|^2$$which implies that $g$ is a complex number of modulus one ($|g|^2=1$), therefore we can write $g=e^{i\varphi}$. Hence $U(1)$ is the group of complex numbers of unit modulus with multiplication as the group operation. We can prove this using the definition of a group. $g=1$ acts as the identity element for this group. The product of two complex numbers with unit modulus also has unit modulus, so we satisfy $x,y\in G\implies x\circ y\in G$. Complex numbers are associative under multiplication, so we satisfy associativity automatically. For $g=e^{i\varphi}$ we write $g^{-1}=e^{-i\varphi}$ and we see that $g\circ g^{-1}=e^{i\varphi}e^{-i\varphi}=e^0=1$, satisfying the inverse requirement for a group. Hence $U(1)$ is indeed a group under multiplication. We saw that a map from $\Re$ to $U(1)$ by writing $g=e^{i\varphi}$ is not an isomorphism. That is, we cannot simply do calculus on $\Re$ and map that to $U(1)$, this is an example of a manifold. When $\varphi\to0$ we can approximate the exponential map to linear order to get:$$\Huge z\rightarrow(1+i\varphi)z$$Note that this approximation is tangent to $U(1)$ at $g=1$. We can try to reconstruct finite elements of $U(1)$ by repeated infinitesimal transformations. Considering $(1+i\varphi/n)^n$ as our map, we see that as $n\to\infty$ the map is reconstructed. More formally:$$\Huge\begin{align}
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+i\varphi/n)^n&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(i\varphi)^k}{n^k}{n\choose k}\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(i\varphi)^k}{n^k}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!k!}\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(i\varphi)^k}{k!}=e^{i\varphi}
\end{align}$$where we have used the fact that the term $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!n^k}$ will tend to one in the limiting case.

## $SU(2)$
We now aim to generalise the above notions to matrices, particularly $M_n(\mathbb{C})$. To do this we must recall [[Index notation|Einstein's summation notation]] as well as the Hermitian conjugate $(A^\dagger)_{ij}=(\bar A)_{ji}$.  We can write the trace and determinant of a matrix using index notation:$$\Huge\begin{align}
\det A&=\epsilon_{i_1i_2,\dots,i_n}A_{1i_1}A_{2i_2}\dots A_{ni_n}\\
\text{Tr }A&=A_{ii}
\end{align}$$where $\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita symbol, a completely antisymmetric tensor. We can then define the general unitary groups:$$\Huge\begin{align}
U(n)&=\{g\in M_n(\mathbb{C}):g^\dagger g=gg^\dagger=\mathbb{1}\}\\
SU(n)&=\{g\in U(n):\det g=1\}
\end{align}$$Focusing on $SU(2)$ we can check if this is indeed a group wrt matrix multiplication:$$\Huge\begin{align}
e&=\mathbb{1}\implies g\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1}g=g,\,\,\forall g\in SU(2)\\
\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^\dagger&=\mathbb{1}^\dagger\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1}\implies e\in\ SU(2)\\
g,g'\in SU(2)&\implies(gg')^\dagger gg'=g'^\dagger g^\dagger gg'=g'^\dagger\mathbb{1}g'=\mathbb{1}\\
\det(gg')&=\det g\deg g'=1\times1=1\\
g\in SU(2)&\implies (g^{-1})^\dagger g^{-1}=(g^\dagger)^\dagger g^{-1}=gg^{-1}=1\\
\det(g^{-1})&=\frac{1}{\det g}=1
\end{align}$$And as matrix multiplication is associative, we have that $SU(2)$ is indeed a group. Note that we can think of $U(2)$ as the group of transformations $U$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$, $\underline z\rightarrow U\underline z=\underline w$, which will leave the inner product $|\underline z|$ invariant:$$\Huge |\underline z|=\begin{pmatrix}\bar z_1&\bar z_2\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}\begin{pmatrix}z_1\\z_2\end{pmatrix}=\underline z^\dagger\cdot\underline z$$so when we apply the transformation:$$\Huge \begin{align}
\underline z&\rightarrow U\underline z\\
\underline z^\dagger&\rightarrow(U\underline z)^\dagger=\underline z^\dagger U^\dagger\\
\underline z^\dagger\underline z&\rightarrow \underline z^\dagger U^\dagger U\underline z=\underline z^\dagger\underline z
\end{align}$$so we see that the inner product is indeed unchanged. We can also take the notion of parametrisation we saw in $U(1)$ to $U(2)$. 

For any invertible matrix in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ we can write:$$\Huge g=\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\implies g^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det g}\begin{pmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{pmatrix}$$Now as $g\in SU(2)$ we have $\det g=1$ and $g^{-1}=g^\dagger$, so:$$\Huge \begin{pmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar a & \bar c \\ \bar b & \bar d\end{pmatrix}$$So the most general matrix in $SU(2)$ can be written as:$$\Huge g=\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ -\bar b & \bar  a\end{pmatrix}$$now $\det g=1\implies|a|^2+|b|^2=1$. As these are complex numbers, we write $a=x_1+ix_2,b=x_3+ix_4$, so we have:$$\Huge SU(2)=\{\underline{x}\in\Re^4:x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2=1\}$$This is the defining equation of the three sphere. That is, $SU(2)$ is a space that looks like a three sphere $S^3$ and we have defined an embedding of $S^3$ into $\Re^4$.

### Pauli matrices:
When working with $U(1)$ we saw that we could write the group using the complex exponential. We aim to introduce a similar notion by writing:$$\Huge g=e^{iA}$$where $A$ is a matrix and $g\in SU(2)$. The exponential of a matrix has to be defined using the series definition:$$\Huge e^{iA}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(iA)^k}{k!}$$Firstly we impose unitarity ($g^\dagger=g^{-1}$), as $(A^n)^\dagger=(A^\dagger)^n$ we have:$$\Huge g^\dagger=e^{-iA^\dagger},\,\,g^{-1}=e^{-iA}\implies A^\dagger=A$$we call such matrices "Hermitian". We must now find the determinant of $e^{iA}$:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
\det g&=\det e^{iA}\\
&=\det\lim_{n\to \infty}\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{iA}{n}\right)^n\\
&=\lim_{n\to \infty}\det\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{iA}{n}\right)^n\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\det\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{iA}{n}\right)\right)^n
\end{align*}$$The determinant can be written explicitly as:$$\Huge\det\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{iA}{n}\right)=\left(1+\frac{iA_{11}}{n}\right)\left(1+\frac{iA_{22}}{n}\right)-\frac{i^2A_{21}A_{12}}{n^2}$$for $A\in M_2(\mathbb{C})$. Expanding this shows:$$\Huge\det\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{iA}{n}\right)=1+\frac{i\,\text{tr}(A)}{n}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})$$where in the limiting case, all terms of order greater than $n^{-1}$ are subleading, so we have:$$\Huge\det g=\det e^{iA}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\left(1+\frac{i\text{ tr}(A)}{n}\right)^n=e^{i\text{ tr(A)}}$$The restriction of $\det g=1$ implies that this RHS term is $1$:$$\Huge e^{i\text{ tr}(A)}=1\implies i\text{ tr}(A)=0\implies\text{tr}(A)=0$$Writing $g=e^{iA}$ implies that $A\in T$ where:$$\Huge T=\{A:A^\dagger=A,\text{ tr}(A)=0\}$$note that for $A,B\in T$ we have $aA+bB\in T$ for $a,b\in\Re$. That is, $T$ is a [[Vector space definitions|vector space]] over the reals. We make the following choice of basis for $T$:$$\Huge\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0 & -i \\ i & 0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$$Such matrices are known as the "Pauli matrices". One can show that these matrices satisfy:$$\Huge[\sigma_i,\sigma_j]=2i\sigma_{ijk}\sigma_k$$where $[a,b]$ is the [[Commutators and Uncertainty Principle|commutator]]. Note that $SU(2)$ is a non-abelian Lie group. We can form group elements of $SU(2)$ by exponentiating arbitrary real linear combinations of the Pauli matrices, that is:$$\Huge g(\underline{\alpha})=e^{i\alpha_j\sigma_j}=e^{i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}$$for $\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)$. As $\alpha_k\sigma_k$ is traceless and Hermitian for any real $\underline{\alpha}$ we have that $g\in SU(2)$, so we get a group element for every vector $\underline{\alpha}\in\Re^3$. Note that this cannot be an injective map as $\Re^3$ is not the same as $S^3$.

We now aim to find the coordinates defined by $\alpha_k$ in order to connect the idea that $SU(2)\cong S^3$. First, consider:$$\Huge g((\alpha_1,0,0))=e^{i\alpha_1\sigma_1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(i\alpha_1\sigma_1)^k}{k!}=\sum_{k=\text{even}}+\sum_{k=\text{odd}}$$now as $\sigma_i^2=\mathbb{1}$, all of the matrix powers in the sum over even $k$ equal $\mathbb{1}$ and all of the matrix powers in the odd sum equal $\sigma_1$:$$\Huge e^{i\alpha_1\sigma_1}=\sum_{k=\text{even}}\frac{(i\alpha_1)^k}{k!}\mathbb{1}+\sum_{k=\text{odd}}\frac{(i\alpha_1)^k}{k!}\sigma_1=\cos(\alpha_1)\mathbb{1}+i\sin(\alpha_1)\sigma_1$$This can be though of as a generalisation of Euler's formula. It is clear to see the non-injectivity of the map as $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_1+2\pi$ will map to the same element. As $\sigma_1$ is self commutating:$$\Huge g((\alpha_1,0,0))g((\alpha_1',0,0))=e^{i\alpha_1\sigma_1}e^{i\alpha_1'\sigma_1}=g((\alpha_1+\alpha_1',0,0))$$so that the matrices of this form are a subgroup of $SU(2)$. Such subgroup works in the same way as $U(1)$ parametrised as $e^{i\phi}$ setting $\alpha_1=\phi$. More formally, these groups are [[Groups#Isomorphism|isomorphic]].

We propose that there is an injective group homomorphism $U(1)\rightarrow SU(2)$, the image of which is a $U(1)$ subgroup of $SU(2)$. One can show that this is true for any subsets of $\underline{\alpha}$ with $\underline{\alpha}=t\alpha_0$, so there are infinitely many $U(1)$ subgroups of $SU(2)$.

We define such subgroups $G_\alpha$ of $SU(2)$ as:$$\Huge G_\alpha=\{e^{it\alpha\sigma}:t\in\Re\}$$for some fixed $\alpha$. This is known as the one-parameter subgroup generated by $\alpha$. A nice property of our definition is that it is easy to find infinitesimal elements using a Taylor expansion. When $\underline{\alpha}$ is small, we write:$$\Huge g(\underline{\alpha})\approx\mathbb{1}+i\alpha_j\sigma_j=\mathbb{1}+i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}$$That is, the space of tangent vectors at $g=\mathbb{1}$ is spanned by $i\sigma_j$ and a general vector is written as $i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}$. $SU(2)$ can then be reconstructed by an infinite iteration of infinitesimal elements:$$\Huge g(\underline{\alpha})=\lim_{n\to \infty}\left(\mathbb{1}+\frac{i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}{n}\right)^n=e^{i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}$$Moreover we can think of recovering any infinitesimal generator $i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}$ by considering the path:$$\Huge s(t)=e^{it\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}$$through $SU(2)$ and taking a derivative wrt $t$ evaluated at $t=0$ (corresponding to $\mathbb{1}\in SU(2)$):$$\Huge \frac{\partial s(t)}{\partial t}|_{t=0}=\frac{\partial }{\partial t}e^{it\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}|_{t=0}=i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}$$It remains to check the surjectivity of this map from $i\underline{\alpha}\underline{\sigma}$ to $SU(2)$. To do this we define the anti-commutator $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$:$$\Huge \{A,B\}=AB+BA$$Now we examine every point on $S^3$ to show that $SU(2)$ is in the image of the map. Writing the general group element as:$$\Huge g=\begin{pmatrix}x_1+ix_2 & x_3+ix_4 \\ -x_3+ix_4 & x_1-ix_2\end{pmatrix}$$where $x_i\in\Re$ and $x_1^2+\dots+x_4^2=1$, we note that $x_1\in[-1,1]$. Fixing $x_1$ will give a slice of $S^3$ that is a two-sphere of radius $\sqrt{1-x_1^2}$. Comparing this to the form of $g(\underline{\alpha})$ we can always choose $a$ such that $\cos(a)=x_1$. This is not unique, however this always exists and is satisfied for any $x_1$. The other variables are then mapped as:$$\Huge \begin{pmatrix}x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\sin a}{a}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_3 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_1\end{pmatrix}$$The question now becomes: Can we always find $\underline{\alpha}$ such that this equation is satisfied for every $x_i$ subject to $x_1^2+\dots+x_4^2=1$. As we have already fixed $x_1=\cos a$, we can restrict the length of $\underline{\alpha}$:$$\Huge |\underline{\alpha}|^2=\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\alpha_3^2=a^2$$which is also a two-sphere. The points on this sphere are mapped to the two-sphere:$$\Huge x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2=1-x_1^2$$by the map defined above, which is one-to-one:$$\large x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2=1-x_1^2=1-\cos^2a=\sin^2a=\frac{\sin^2a}{a^2}(\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\alpha_3^2)$$hence we have shown that every element of $SU(2)$ can be written as:$$\Huge g(\underline{\alpha})=e^{i\underline{\alpha}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}$$where $\sigma_j$ are a basis for infinitesimal transformations. 

### Core ideas from example:
> We defined a continuous group by demanding that its action on vectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$ leaves the inner form (vector norm) invariant.
> $SU(2)$ does not have a one-to-one map to $\Re^3$, it has a non-trivial topology and can be identified with the three-sphere $S^3$.
> We found that infinitesimal transformations are tangent to the group at the identity element, so group elements can be recovered by iterating infinitesimal transformations in a limit. This is the same as exponentiating the element and gives a surjective map to $SU(2)$. This allows us to do computations in terms of Pauli matrices $\sigma_i$ instead of the group $SU(2)$.

## $SO(3)$ versus $SU(2)$:

The group $SO(3)$ is the group $S\in M_3(\Re)$ such that $S^T=S^{-1}$ and $\det S=1$. We will explore the global structure of $SO(3)$ by examining a map from $SU(2)$ to $SO(3)$. Take the components of a vector $\underline{v}\in\Re^3$ and arrange them as such:$$\Huge M_\underline{v}=\begin{pmatrix}v_3 & v_1-iv_2 \\ v_1+iv_2 & -v_3\end{pmatrix}$$Note that addition of vectors in $\Re^3$ becomes matrix addition of $M_\underline{v}$ and scalar multiplication of $\underline{v}$ becomes multiplication of $M_\underline{v}$ by the same constant. Consider the map:$$\Huge F(g):M_\underline{v}\rightarrow gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger=F(g)[M_\underline{v}]$$for $g\in SU(2)$. That is, we have defined a map $F(g)$ to $\Re^3$ for each element $g\in SU(2)$. We propose that this is a group homomorphism.

To prove this, we must first check that $F(g)$ preserves the structure of $M_\underline{v}$. Note that $M_\underline{v}$ is the most general matrix in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with $M_\underline{v}^\dagger=M_\underline{v}$ and $\text{Tr}(M_\underline{v})=0$. Under $F(g)$ it is mapped to $gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger$, which must obey:$$\Huge (gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger)^\dagger=(g^\dagger)^\dagger M_\underline{v}^\dagger g^\dagger=gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger$$and:$$\Huge \text{Tr}(gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger)=g_{ij}(M_\underline{v})_{jk}g_{ki}^\dagger=g_{ki}^\dagger g_{ij}(M_\underline{v})_{jk}=\text{Tr}(g^\dagger gM_\underline{v})=\text{Tr}(M_\underline{v})=0$$as $g^\dagger g=\mathbb{1}$ because $g\in SU(2)$. Now notice that $F(g)$ is a linear map on the coordinates of $\Re^3$ as:$$\Huge g(M_\underline{v}+M_{\underline{v'}})g^\dagger=gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger+gM_\underline{v'}g^\dagger$$Therefore, $F$ can be written as:$$\Huge F(g):\underline{v}\rightarrow\Phi(g)\underline{v}$$for some $\Phi(g)\in M_3(\Re)$. Next observe that $\underline{v}\cdot\underline{v}=-\det M_\underline{v}$, which is mapped to:$$\Huge \det M_\underline{v}\rightarrow\det(g M_\underline{v}g^\dagger)=\det g\det M_\underline{v}\det g^{-1}=\det M_\underline{v}$$Hence the inner product is left invariant. As the map is linear, it must map to $O(3)$. $F$ is continuous, which maps $\mathbb{1}\in SU(2)$ to $\mathbb{1}\in O(3)$. As $SU(2)$ is connected, it only maps to the elements of $O(3)$ connected with the identity, which is precisely $SO(3)$.

Hence we have a way for each $g\in SU(2)$ to be assigned an element of $SO(3)$ by sending $g$ to $F(g)$, or equivalently $\Phi(g)$. Composition of maps in $SO(3)$ is therefore the same as composing elements in $SU(2)$:$$\Huge F(g'g)(M_\underline{v})=g'gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger g'^\dagger=F(g')[F(g)[M_\underline{v}]]$$so that $F$ is a group homomorphism.

Note that the homomorphism is not injective, as we have $F(g)=F(-g)$. We can see that they map to the same action on $\Re^3$:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
F(g)&:M_\underline{v}\rightarrow gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger\\
F(-g)&:M_\underline{v}\rightarrow(-g)M_\underline{v}(-g)^\dagger=gM_\underline{v}g^\dagger
\end{align*}$$Therefore, $g=-\mathbb{1}$ is in the kernel of $F$:$$\Huge F(-\mathbb{1})[M_\underline{v}]=-\mathbb{1}M_\underline{v}(-\mathbb{1})^\dagger=M_\underline{v}$$which is $\mathbb{1}\in SO(3)$. We then ask if the map is even surjective. Consider how a one-parameter subgroup of $SU(2)$ is mapped:$$\Huge g(0,0,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}=e^{i\theta\sigma_3}$$We then have:$$\large\begin{align*}
g(0,0,\theta)M_\underline{v}g(0,0,\theta)^\dagger&=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta} & 0\\
0 & e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_3 & v_1-iv_2\\
v_1+iv_2 & -v_3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta} & 0\\
0 & e^{i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}v_3 & e^{2i\theta}(v_1-iv_2)\\
e^{-2i\theta}(v_1+iv_2) & -v_3\end{pmatrix}\\
e^{2i\theta}(v_1-iv_2)&=(\cos(2\theta)+i\sin(2\theta))(v_1-iv_2)\\
&=v_1\cos(2\theta)+v_2\sin(2\theta)-i(v_2\cos(2\theta)-v_1\sin(2\theta))
\end{align*}$$So we see that the map sends elements:$$\large\begin{pmatrix}v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}v_1\cos(2\theta)+v_2\sin(2\theta) \\ v_2\cos(2\theta)-v_1\sin(2\theta) \\ v_3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\theta) & \sin(2\theta) & 0 \\ -\sin(2\theta) & \cos(2\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3\end{pmatrix}$$That is to say, using the fact that the map is linear, the matrix associated with the linear map $F(g)$ is:$$\Huge\Phi(g(0,0,\theta))=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\theta) & \sin(2\theta) & 0 \\ -\sin(2\theta) & \cos(2\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$Therefore we see that $g$ maps to a rotation around the $v_3$ axis by $-2\theta$. Similarly, one can show that rotations around other axis are generated by the other group elements of $SU(2)$. That is, for a rotation around $\underline {v_0}$ simply use $e^{i\theta\underline{v_0}\cdot\underline{\sigma}}$ where $\underline{v_0}\cdot\underline{v_0}=1$.

If we can write any element of $SO(3)$ as a composition of rotations around fixed axis, we have hence proven that the homomorphism from $SU(2)$ to $SO(3)$ is surjective. This is indeed true as the proposition shows:

Every element $R$ of $SO(3)$ can be written as a product of three rotations $R_{\underline{v_i}}(\phi_i)$ around fixed axis $\underline{v_i}$ by angles $\phi_i$:$$\Huge R=R_{\underline{v_1}}(\phi_1)R_{\underline{v_2}}(\phi_2)R_{\underline{v_3}}(\phi_3)$$

We have seen that this surjective map has $\mathbb{1}$ in its kernel. We propose that the kernel makes up the [[Families of groups#Cyclic groups|cyclic group]] with two elements $\mathbb{Z}_2=\{\mathbb{1},-\mathbb{1}\}\in SU(2)$. As there are exactly two points in $SU(2)$ that map to each point in $SO(3)$, we call the map a double covering, we write:$$\Huge SO(3)\cong SU(2)/\mathbb{Z}_2\cong\,S/\mathbb{Z}_2$$where $\mathbb{Z}_2$ acts by identifying antipodal points on the three-sphere. Proof:
>We have shown that the map is a non-injective homomorphism with kernel $\{\mathbb{1},-\mathbb{1}\}$ and that any rotation around a fixed axis is in the image. As any element in $SO(3)$ can be written as a product of three rotations, any element of $SO(3)$ is in the image of this homomorphism. That is to say, the map is surjective. We have also seen that $SU(2)$ is isomorphic to $S^3$.
>Antipodal points on $S^3$ correspond to sending $x_i\rightarrow-x_i$ for any solution to $x_1^2+\dots+x_4^2=1$. We can see that $g$ and $-g$ in $SU(2)$ correspond to antipodal points on $S^3$, and are hence antipodal on $SU(2)$. We have seen that $g$ and $-g$ are mapped to the same element on $SO(3)$. Hence if we identify $g$ and $-g$ in $SU(2)$, the group homomorphism becomes a group isomorphism. Finally, as the map from $SU(2)$ to $SO(3)$ is surjective, we conclude that $SO(3)\cong SU(2)/\mathbb{Z}_2\cong\,S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$.

Note that the group $SO(3)$ of rotations in $R^3$ is not a two-sphere, as there are nontrivial rotations that leave $\underline{v}$ invariant. These correspond to $U(1)=S^1$, which combines with $S^2$ to form $S^3/\mathbb{Z}_2$. 

#### Topological comparison:
Consider closed loops $\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow G$ such that $\phi(0)=\phi(1)$. For $SU(2)$ there are no non-trivial maps, any closed loop in $S^3$ can be shrunk to a point. Such spaces are called simply connected.

Now consider the path from $\mathbb{1}$ to $-\mathbb{1}$ in $SU(2)$. Under $F$ this maps to a closed path in $SO(3)$ that starts and ends at $\mathbb{1}$. If this curve is continuously deformed, it will still lift to an open curve in $SU(2)$, although it may now go from $g$ to $-g$ in $SU(2)$, which means there is no way of shrinking it to a point. Therefore $SO(3)$ is not simply connected. Consider looping twice around any loop in $SO(3)$, which can be lifted to any closed curve in $SU(2)$, which we know can be shrunk to a point. We have hence shown that the fundamental group of $SO(3)$ contains a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ element. For a given manifold with non-trivial fundamental group, there is a unique way to find a covering space that is simply connected. Here, $SU(2)$ is the universal cover of $SO(3)$.
