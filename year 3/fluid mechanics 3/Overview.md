
By defining fluids as a continuum of particles and systematically adding physical laws/constraints, we can model increasingly complex behaviour:
> The [[Kinematics of Fluids#What is a fluid?|continuum hypothesis]] defines a fluid as a continuous substance described by a density field $\rho(\underline{x})$ and a velocity field $\underline{u}(\underline{x})$. We adopt two perspectives to describe these fields motion:
> > The Eulerian perspective, where we observe fluid flow through a fixed patch of space.
> > The Lagrangian perspective, where we "flow" with the fluid.
> We define the [[Kinematics of Fluids#The material derivative|material derivative]], $D/Dt$, as the bridge between these perspectives, allowing for the calculation of the rate of change of a material property in the fluid following a particle.
> We then apply the following physical constraints:
> > Conservation of mass leads to the [[Kinematics of Fluids#Conservation of mass|continuity equation]]. Note that for most fluids, this is simplified assuming incompressibility $\underline{\nabla}\cdot\underline{u}=0$.
> > Defining [[Kinematics of Fluids#Vorticity|vorticity]], $\underline{\omega}=\underline{\nabla}\times\underline{u}$, as the curl of the velocity field, we often impose that $\underline{\omega}=0$ (irrotational) for many systems. Note that we can also calculate the [[Kinematics of Fluids#Circulation|circulation]], $\Gamma$, for non-zero vorticity systems as the integral of velocity around a closed curve.
> > Assuming both irrotationality and incompressibility, we further impose that the velocity field can be defined as the gradient of a [[Kinematics of Fluids#Potential flows|velocity potential]] $\phi$. This definition reduces problems to simply solving [[Laplace's equation]].
> Moving on from kinematic definitions, we investigate the dynamics of fluids by defining the forces acting on a fluid, described in the [[Dynamics of ideal fluids#Incompressible Euler equations|Euler equations]].
> Building off of the Euler equations, we define the energy head $H$ as a constant quantity along streamlines in [[Dynamics of ideal fluids#Bernoulli's principle|Bernoulli's principle]].
> Putting our work to use, we model [[Water waves|water waves]] using a potential flow and study the dynamics of the free surface. From this, we define concepts such as the [[Water waves#Dispersion relation|dispersion relation]].

These all work well for a fluid under ideal assumptions, however this is not enough for us. We therefore change certain aspects of our model:
> By analysing a free surface in equilibrium, we find that small perturbations can lead to exponential growth and destabilisation in the free surface. This can happen in the [[Instability#The Rayleigh-Taylor instability|Rayleigh-Taylor]] and [[Instability#The Kelvin-Helmholtz instability|Kelvin-Helmholtz]] instabilities.
> By allowing for [[Compressible flow|compressible flow]], we can now model gasses. This naturally introduces the notion of [[Compressible flow#Sound waves|sound waves]] as small-amplitude pressure oscillations and [[Compressible flow#Nonlinearity|shocks]].
> We can introduce internal friction of the fluid via a [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#Stress tensor symmetry|stress tensor]]. This leads to the [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#The Navier-Stokes equations|Navier-Stokes equations]], which are the defining equations for viscous fluid motion.
> Introducing viscosity changes how a system evolves, specifically through the [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#Diffusion of vorticity|diffusion of vorticity]] as well as [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#Viscous dissipation|energy dissipation]].
> To better understand the effects of viscosity, we "nondimensionalise" the Navier-Stokes equations by defining appropriate speed and length scales. This leads to the definition of the [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#The Reynolds number|Reynolds number]], $\text{Re}$.
> Looking at high and low $\text{Re}$ limits, we define [[Dynamics of viscous fluids#Stokes flows|Stokes flows]] for $\text{Re}<<1$. This limit reduces the problem to solving linear PDEs
