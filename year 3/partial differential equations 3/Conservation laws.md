
We will now consider a special type of [[Introduction to PDEs#Quasi-linear PDEs|quasi-linear]] first order PDEs that describe conserved quantities such as mass and charge. The general form for scalar conservation laws for functions is:$$\Huge\partial_tu(\underline{x},t)+\text{div}_{\underline{x}}\underline{f}(u(\underline{x},t))=0$$where $u:\Re^n\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\Re$ and $\underline{f}:\Re\rightarrow\Re^n$. Here, $\underline{f}$ is known as the flux function. Note that:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
\text{div}_\underline{x}\underline{f}(u(\underline{x},t))&=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\partial }{\partial x_j}f_j(u(\underline{x},t))\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^n \frac{df_i}{du}(u(\underline{x},t))\partial_{x_j}u(\underline{x},t)\\
&=\underline{f}'(u(\underline{x},t))\cdot\underline{\nabla}_{\underline{x}}u(\underline{x},t)
\end{align*}$$More generally, systems of conservation laws for vector-valued functions $\underline{u}=\underline{u}(\underline{x},t)$ have the form:$$\Huge \partial_t\underline{u}(\underline{x},t)+\text{div}_{\underline{x}}\underline{F}(\underline{u}(\underline{x},t))=0$$where $\underline{u}:\Re^n\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\Re^m$ and $\underline{F}:\Re^m\rightarrow\Re^{m\times n}$ is a matrix-valued function with components $\underline{F}(\underline{u})_{ij}=f_{ij}(\underline{u})$ for $i\in\{1,\dots,m\},j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$. The divergence term is a vector and its $i$-th component is the divergence of the $i$-th row of $\underline{F}(\underline{u}(\underline{x,t}))$:$$\large(\text{div}_\underline{x}\underline{F}(\underline{u}(\underline{x},t)))_i=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\partial }{\partial x_j}f_{ij}(\underline{u}(\underline{x},t))=\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{\partial f_{ij}}{\partial u_k}(\underline{u}(\underline{x},t))\partial_{x_k}u_k(\underline{x},t)$$

Take for example Euler's continuity equation:$$\Huge\begin{cases}\partial_tu(\underline{x},t)+\text{div}_\underline{x}(\underline{\beta}(\underline{x})u(\underline{x},t))=0&x\in\Omega \\
u(\underline{x},0)=u_0(\underline{x})&x\in\partial\Omega\end{cases}$$with the no flux condition $\underline{\beta}(\underline{x})\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}(\underline{x})=0$ where $\underline{\hat{n}}$ is the unit normal to $\partial\Omega$. We claim that if $u,\beta,\Omega$ are smooth then:$$\Huge\int_\Omega u(\underline{x},t)d\underline{x}=\int_\Omega u_0(\underline{x})d\underline{x}$$It is enough to show that $\partial_t\int_\Omega u(\underline{x},t)d\underline{x}=0$ as:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
\partial_t\int_\Omega u(\underline{x},t)d\underline{x}&=\int_\Omega\partial_tu(\underline{x},t)d\underline{x}\\
&=\int_\Omega\text{div}_\underline{x}(\underline{\beta}(\underline{x})u(\underline{x},t))d\underline{x}\\
&=\int_{\partial\Omega}\underline{\beta}(\underline{x})u(\underline{x},t)\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}
(\underline{x})d\underline{x}=0\end{align*}$$

Consider cars on a straight single lane, parametrised by $x\in\Re$. We denote $u(x,t)$ as the density of cars at point $x$ and time $t$. We denote $\phi(x,t)$ the flux of cars at point $x$ and time $t$, that is the ratio at which cars pass point $x$ at time $t$. As no cars can be created or destroyed, for any $-\infty<a<b<\infty$ and $0<t_1<t_2<\infty$ we have:$$\Huge\int_a^bu(x,t_2)dx+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\phi(a,t)-\phi(b,t)dt=\int_a^bu(x,t_1)dx$$That is to say, the number of cars on $[a,b]$ at time $t_2$ plus the number of cars that entered at $x=a$ minus the number of cars that left at $x=b$ between times $t_1,t_2$ is equal to the number of cars on $[a,b]$ at $t_1$. We can write this as:$$\Huge\int_a^bu(x,t_2)-u(x,t_1)dx=-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\phi(b,t)-\phi(a,t)dt$$If $u,\phi\in C^1$ we can write the above as:$$\Huge \int_a^b\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\partial_x(x,t)dt\,dx=-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_a^b\partial_x\phi(x,t)dx\,dt$$Then by Fubini's theorem we can change the order of integration:$$\Huge\iint_{[a,b]\times[t_1,t_2]}\partial_tu(x,t)+\partial_x\phi(x,t)\,dx\,dt=0$$Since the above holds for any $a<b,t_1<t_2$ and $u,\phi\in C^1$ we must have that:$$\Huge \partial_t u(x,t)+\partial_x\phi(x,t)=0$$If $\phi=f(u)$ then this is equivalent to:$$\Huge \partial_tu(x,t)+f'(u(x,t))\partial_xu(x,t)=0$$
## Transport equation example:
Take for example the transport equation defined by:$$\Huge \partial_tu(x,t)+c\partial_xu(x,t)=0$$where $c\in\Re$ is a conservation law with $f(u)=cu$. We focus on the conservation law:$$\Huge\begin{cases}\partial_tu(x,t)+\partial_x(f(u(x,t)))=0&(x,t)\in\Re\times(0,\infty) \\
u(x,0)=u_0(x)&x\in\Re\end{cases}$$Assuming the differentiability of $f$, the first line reads $\partial_tu(x,t)+c(u(x,t))\partial_xu(x,t)=0$. By definition, any conservation law a [[Introduction to PDEs#Quasi-linear PDEs|quasi-linear]] first order PDE and as such we can use the [[The Method of Characteristics#General setting|method of characteristics]] to solve it. Here we have the domain $\Omega=\Re\times(0,\infty)$ and the Cauchy curve $\Gamma=\Re\times\{0\}$, parametrised by $\underline{\gamma}(s)=(x_0(s),t_0(s))=(s,0)$ with $s\in\Re$. The leading vector field is then $\underline{a}(x,t,u)=(c(u),1)$ with source term $b(x,t,u)=0$. Now for any $s\in\Re$:$$\large\underline{a}(x_0(s),t_0(s),u(x_0(s),t_0(s)))\cdot(t_0'(s)-x_0'(s))=(c(u(s,0)),1)\cdot(0,-1)=-1\neq0$$we conclude that all points on the Cauchy curve are non-characteristic and that we can use the local existence and uniqueness [[The Method of Characteristics#Local existence and uniqueness with continuous dependence in parameters|result]].

## Finding characteristics for conservation laws with one spatial variable:
We are now considering the system of ODEs:$$\Huge\begin{cases}\partial_\tau X(\tau,s)=c(z(\tau,s)) \\
\partial_\tau T(\tau,s)=1 \\
\partial_\tau z(\tau,s)=0\end{cases},\begin{cases}X(0,s)=s \\
T(0,s)=0 \\
z(0,s)=u(x_0(s),t_0(s))=u(s,0)=u_0(s)\end{cases}$$for $s\in\Re$. The ODEs for $T,z$ are immediately solvable and we find:$$\Huge T(\tau,s)=T(0,s)+\tau=\tau$$which intuitively makes sense as the $t$ variable governs the characteristic flow and can be thought of as time. We now have that $X$ satisfies:$$\Huge\begin{cases}\partial_\tau X(\tau,s)=c(u_0(s)) \\
X(0,s)=s\end{cases}$$for $s\in\Re$. We can therefore conclude:$$\Huge X(\tau,s)=X(0,s)+c(u_0(s))\tau=s+c(u_0(s))\tau=s+c(u_0(s))T(\tau,s)$$An important observation to make is that the characteristics are always given as straight lines.

Continuing with the method of characteristics, we now need to invert the flow:$$\Huge (\tau,s)\rightarrow(X(\tau,s),T(\tau,s))$$Using our formula for $X(\tau,s)$ we have an implicit equation for $s$ in terms of $X,T$. We ask under which conditions can we guarantee that we can find $s$?

To answer this, consider the function $h^{(t)}:\Re\rightarrow\Re$ defined by:$$\Huge h^{(t)}(s)=s+c(u_0(s))t$$where $t\in[0,\infty)$ is fixed. We are essentially solving $x=h^{(t)}(s)$ for $s$, and so must understand when $h^{(t)}$ is invertible. In one dimension, invertibility for continuous functions is connected to strict monotonicity. Assuming that $c,u_0$ are differentiable on $\Re$ we see that:$$\Huge \frac{d}{ds}h^{(t)}(s)=1+\partial_s(c(u_0(s)))t>_{t=0}1$$That is, $h^{(0)}$ is strictly increasing and we can invert the flow from the image of $h^{(0)}$. Continuing on the above we find that when $c,u_0$ are continuously differentiable $\Re$ it is natural to consider $t-s$, potentially very close to $t=0$. We see that this derivative is positive if:
> $t\in[0,\infty)$ when $\partial_s(c(u_0(s)))\geq0$
> $0\leq t<-\frac{1}{\partial_s(c(u_0(s)))}$ when $\partial_s(c(u_0(s)))<0$

# Local existence/characteristics for conservation laws on an interval:
This leads us to the next observation, written as a lemma. Let $c,\in C^1, u_0\in C^1([a,b])$. For any $-\infty<a<b<\infty$ we define:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
I_{(a,b)}&=\{s\in(a,b):\partial_sc(u_0(s))<0\}\\
t_c^{(a,b)}&=\inf_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}\left(-\frac{1}{\partial_sc(u_0(s))}\right)=\inf_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}\left(-\frac{1}{c'(u_0(s))u_0'(s)}\right)\\
\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}&=\{(x,t)\in\Re\times(0,t_c^{(a,b)}):a+c(u_0(a))t<x<b+c(u_0(b))t\}
\end{align*}$$with the convention that $t_c^{(a,b)}=\infty$ if $I_{(a,b)}$ is empty. This essentially defines the domain where the function $h^{(t)}(s)$ defined above is invertible. Then $t_c^{(a,b)}>0$ and as such $\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}\neq\emptyset$ and there exists a continuously differentiable function $u:\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}\rightarrow\Re$ satisfying the conservation law:$$\Huge\begin{cases}\partial_tu(x,t)+c(u(x,t))\partial_xu(x,t)=0&(x,t)\in\mathcal{D_{(a,b)}} \\
u(x,0)=u_0(x)&x\in(a,b)\end{cases}$$Moreover, $u$ satisfies the implicit equation:$$\Huge u(x,t)=u_0(x-c(u(x,t))t),\,\,(x,t)\in\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}$$Furthermore, the characteristics for the conservation law are straight lines and $u$ is constant along characteristics. In particular, for each $s\in(a,b)$, the characteristic that passes through $(s,0)$ is the line in the $(x,t)$ plane with slope $1/c(u_0(s))$.

## Proof:
When $I_{(a,b)}\neq\emptyset$, we have that:$$\Huge t_c^{(a,b)}=\inf_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}\left(-\frac{1}{\partial_sc(u_0)(s))}\right)=1/\sup_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}(-\partial_sc(u_0(s)))$$furthermore we have:$$\Huge t_c^{(a,b)}=0\iff\sup_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}(-\partial_sc(u(s)))=\infty$$However from the condition that $c\circ u_0\in C^1([a,b])$ and as $[a,b]$ is obviously compact, we say that $\partial_s(c\circ u_0)(s)$ is bounded on $[a,b]$, which full contains $I_{(a,b)}$. This implies:$$\Huge\sup_{s\in I_{(a,b)}}(-\partial_sc(u_0(s)))\neq\infty\implies t_c^{(a,b)}>0$$
We can now solve the PDE using the method of characteristics. The characteristics and $z(\tau,s)$ are given by:$$\Huge\begin{cases}T(\tau,s)=\tau&s\in(a,b) \\
z(\tau,s)=z(0,s)=u_0(s) \\
X(\tau,s)=s+c(u_0(s))\tau=s+c(u_0(s))T(\tau,s)\end{cases}$$We now invert the flow $(\tau,s)\rightarrow(X(\tau,s),T(\tau,s))$, using the fact we previously found $\tau(x,t)=t$. We find $s(x,t)$ by solving:$$\Huge x=s+c(u_0(s))t=h^{(t)}(s)$$As $c\circ u_0\in C^1([a,b])$, we can differentiate with no problems:$$\Huge \frac{d}{ds}h^{(t)}(s)=1+\partial_sc(u_0((s))t$$Now we restrict $t\in[0,t_c^{(a,b)})$:
> If $s\notin I_{(a,b)}$ then this derivative is positive, as the partial derivative term is not negative.
> If $s\in I_{(a,b)}$ then the partial derivative term itself is negative, however we use the fact that $t<t_c^{(a,b)}$:$$\Huge \frac{d}{ds}h^{(t)}(s)>1+\partial_sc(u_0(s))t_c^{(a,b)}\geq0$$This is because by definition, $t_c^{(a,b)}\leq-1/\partial_sc(u_0(s))$, which we use to show that the term with the critical time is $\geq-1$, making the whole expression positive or zero.

Therefore we can invert $h^{(t)}(s)$ on its image:$$\Huge h^{(t)}(a,b)=(h^{(t)}(a),h^{(t)}(b))=(a+c(u_0(a))t,b+c(u_0(s))t)$$and the inverted flow becomes:$$\Huge \tau(x,t)=t,\,\,s(x,t)=(h^{(t)})^{-1}(x),\,\,(x,t)\in\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}$$Noting that:$$\Huge D_{(\tau,s)}(X,T)=\begin{pmatrix}c(u_0(s)) & 1+\partial_sc(u_0(s))T(\tau,s) \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$is invertible on $\mathcal{D}_{(a,b)}$ we conclude that by the inverse function theorem, that $\tau,s\in C^1$. Finally, we can define:$$\Huge u(x,t)=z(\tau(x,t),s(x,t))=u_0(s(x,t))$$as the solution to the PDE. It remains to show that this holds on the boundary:$$\large x=s(x,0)+\partial_s(c\circ u_0)(s)\cdot0=s(x,0)\implies u(x,0)=u_0(s(x,0))=u_0(x)$$
Writing $x=s+c(u_0(s))t$, or $t=\frac{x-s}{c(u_0(s))}$, it is clear that characteristics are straight lines and $u$ is constant on these characteristics with value $u_0(s)$ if it passes through $(s,0)$. 

Lastly, noting that $u(x,t)=u_0(s(x,t))$, we can write:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
x&=s(x,t)+c(u_0(s(x,t)))t\\
&=s(x,t)+c(u(x,t))t\\
\implies s(x,t)&=x-c(u(x,t))t\\
\implies u(x,t)&=u_0(x-c(u(x,t))t)
\end{align*}$$
Note that the only condition needed to extend this result to $\Re$ is that if $c\circ u_0$ has a bounded derivative (wrt $s$).
