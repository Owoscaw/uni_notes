
# Parametrisations of regular surfaces:

A subset $S$ of $\Re^3$ is called a surface if it can be covered completely by a set of local parametrisations. It is called a regular surface if for every point $\underline{p}\in S$ there exists an open set $V\subset\Re^3$ with $\underline{p}\in V$ and a map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow V\cap S$, where $U$ is an open set of $\Re^2$, such that:
> The map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow V\cap S$ is smooth
> The map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow V\cap S$ is a homeomorphism
> The partial derivatives $\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(u,v),\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(u,v)\in\Re^3$ are linearly independent for all $(u,v)\in U$. As a consequence, the image $d_{(u,v)}\underline{x}(\Re^2)$ (where $d_{(u,v)}$ represents the differential operator) is a two-dimensional Euclidean plane.

The map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow V\cap S$ is called a local parametrisation of $S$ at the point $\underline{p}$, whereas its inverse is called a local coordinate chart of $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

Let $U\subset\Re^2$ be open and $g:U\rightarrow\Re$ be a smooth function. Then the graph of $g$ given by $\{(u,v,g(u,v)\in\Re^3:(u,v)\in\Re^2\}$ is a regular surface in $\Re^3$:
> Let $S$ be equal to this set, then we choose the map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow\Re^3$ given by:$$\Huge \underline{x}(u,v)=(u,v,g(u,v))$$as a global parametrisation of $S$ with $V=\Re^3$. It is obvious that the image of $\underline{x}$ covers every $\underline{p}\in S$, and that the map it smooth. It remains to show the final properties.
> The map $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ is bijective and therefore $\underline{x}^{-1}:S\rightarrow U$ exists. Surjectivity of this inverse is obvious, so we show injectivity:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
\underline{x}(u,v)=\underline{x}(u',v')&\implies (u,v,g(u,v))=(u',v',g(u',v'))\\
&\implies(u,v)=(u',v')
\end{align*}$$Moreover, $\underline{x}^{-1}$ is the restriction of $\pi:\Re^3\rightarrow\Re^2$ with $\pi(u,v,w)=(u,v)$, which is continuous. Therefore the restriction $\underline{x}^{-1}$ is also continuous, making $\underline{x}$ a homeomorphism.
> Since $\underline{x}(u,v)=(u,v,g(u,v))$ we have:$$\Huge \frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(u,v)=\left(1,0,\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}(u,v)\right),\,\,\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(u,v)=\left(0,1,\frac{\partial g}{\partial v}(u,v)\right)$$These partial derivatives are obviously linearly independent, completing the proof.

Consider the following examples:
> Let $U=\Re^2$ and $g(u,v)=\frac{u^2}{a^2}+\frac{v^2}{b^2}$ with $a,b>0$. The graph $S$ defined by $(u,v,g(u,v))$ is called an elliptic paraboloid. The intersection of $S$ with horizontal planes $E_c=\{(u,v,c)\in\Re^3:u,v\in\Re\}$ with $c>0$ leads to ellipses defined by $\frac{u^2}{a^2}+\frac{v^2}{b^2}=c$.
> Let $U=\Re^2$ and $g(u,v)=\frac{u^2}{a^2}-\frac{v^2}{b^2}$ with $a,b>0$. The graph defined by $(u,v,g(u,v))$ is called a hyperbolic paraboloid. The intersection of the graph with horizontal planes $E_c$ is then described by:$$\Huge X\cdot Y=\left(\frac{u}{a}+\frac{v}{b}\right)\left(\frac{u}{a}-\frac{v}{b}\right)=c$$Note that these definitions of $X,Y$ can be viewed as coordinate changes and the level sets of the surface are hyperbolae 

# Regular surfaces as level sets of functions:

We return to the example of the sphere $S(r)\subset\Re^3$ for $r>0$. We have $S(r)=f^{-1}(r^2)$ for $f:\Re^3\rightarrow\Re$ with $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2$. All level sets $f^{-1}(c)$ with $c=r^2>0$ are regular surfaces, except for $c=0$. Note that:$$\Huge\nabla f(x,y,z)=(2x,2y,2z)$$and therefore $\nabla f(0,0,0)=(0,0,0)$. Avoiding values where this is true allows preimages $f^{-1}(c)\subset\Re^3$ to represent regular surfaces. Before we formulate this, we must introduce the following notions:

## Regular points/values:
Let $U\subset\Re^3$ be an open set and $f:U\rightarrow\Re$ be a smooth function. A point $\underline{p}\in U$ is called a regular point of $f$ if:$$\Huge \underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(\underline{p}\right),\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(\underline{p}),\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(\underline{p})\right)\neq0$$Let $c$ be in the range of $f$, that is $c\in f(U)$. We then call $c$ a regular value of $f$ if all points in the preimage $f^{-1}(c)=\{\underline{q}\in U:f(\underline{q})=c\}$ are regular points. That is to say $c\in f(U)$ is a regular value if and only if:$$\Huge \underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})\neq0,\,\,\forall\underline{p}\in f^{-1}(c)$$
A point $\underline{p}\in U$ is called a critical point of $f$ if we have $\underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})=0$ and a value $c\in f(U)$ is called a critical value of $f$ if $f^{-1}(c)$ contains a critical point.

Let $U\subset\Re^3$ be an open set and $f:U\rightarrow\Re$ be a smooth function. Let $c\in f(U)$ be a regular value of $f$. Then the preimage:$$\Huge f^{-1}(c)=\{x\in U:f(x)=c\}$$is a regular surface in $\Re^3$. We first show some examples before proof:
> Let $f:\Re^3\rightarrow\Re$ be given by $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-z^2$ and $S=f^{-1}(1)$. Note that $S$ is non-empty since $f(1,0,0)=1$. Now as:$$\Huge\nabla f(x,y,z)=(2x,2y,-2z)$$the only critical point of $f$ is the origin. Since $f(0,0,0)=0$, $1\in f(\Re^3)$ is a regular value of $f$ and $S$ is a regular surface. 
> We describe the cylinder $S$ as a pre-image of a regular value:$$\Huge S=\{(x,y,z)\in\Re^3:x^2+y^2=1\}$$and we can write $S=f^{-1}(1)$ with $f:\Re^3\rightarrow\Re$ with $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2$. Since $\nabla f(x,y,z)=(2x,2y,0)$, the critical points of $f$ are of the form $(0,0,z)\in\Re^3,z\in\Re$. On these points, $f(0,0,z)=0$ and every $c\in f(\Re^3)\{0\}=(0,\infty)$ is a regular value of $f$.

Now we prove the statement:
> Let $c\in f(U)$ be a regular value of $f$ and $\underline{p}=(u_0,v_0,w_0)\in f^{-1}(c)$. Since $c$ is a regular value, we have:$$\Huge\nabla f(\underline{p})=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\left(\underline{p}\right),\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}(\underline{p}),\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}(\underline{p})\right)\neq0$$Which means at least one of $\nabla f(\underline{p})$ is non-zero. We can assume WLOG $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}(\underline{p})\neq0$. Now we use the implicit function theorem and conclude that there exists an open set $U_0\subset\Re^2$ with $(u_0,_0)\in U_0$ and an open interval $I\subset\Re$ with $w_0\in I$ and a smooth function $g:U_0\rightarrow I$ with $g(u_0,v_0)=w_0$ such that:$$\Huge (U_0\times I)\cap f^{-1}(c)=\{(u,v,g(u,v)):(u,v)\in U_0\}$$This shows that $f^{-1}(c)$ is negative in the local window $U_0\times I\subset U$ about $\underline{p}$. We conclude that $(U_0\times I)\cap f^{-1}(c)$ is a regular surface. Moreover, $x:U_0\rightarrow\Re^3$ given by:$$\Huge x(u,v)=(u,v,g(u,v))$$is a local parametrisation of $f^{-1}(c)$ at $\underline{p}$. Since we can cover all $f^{-1}(c)$ by such local parametrisations, the pre-image $f^{-1}(c)\subset\Re^3$ is a regular surface.

# Change of parametrisations:

A surface $S\subset\Re^3$ is covered by a collection of local parametrisations. Some of these will overlap, meaning there are pairs of parametrisations $x_1:U_1\rightarrow V_1\cap S$ and $x_2:U_2\rightarrow V_2\cap S$ such that $(V_1\cap V_2)\cap S\neq\emptyset$. The points in these overlapping region can then be reached via two different parametrisations and it is natural to ask about the change of one parametrisation to the other, It turns out that such changes are diffeomorphisms. Let us first introduce this relevant notion of a diffeomorphism.

Let $U_1,U_2\subset\Re^n$ be two open sets. A smooth map $h:U_1\rightarrow U_2$ is called a diffeomorphism if it is bijective and if its inverse $h^{-1}:U_2\rightarrow U_1$ is also smooth.

For example, the map $h:\Re\rightarrow(0,\infty)$ with $h(x)=e^x$ is a diffeomorphism since it is smooth and bijective, while its inverse $h^{-1}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\Re$ with $h^{-1}(x)=\log x$ is also smooth.

Let $S\subset\Re^3$ be a regular surface and $x_1:U_1\rightarrow V_1\cap S$ and $x_2:U_2\rightarrow V_2\cap S$ be two local parametrisations. Assume that $W=x_1(U_1)\cap x_2(U_2)$ is not empty ($W=(V_1\cap V_2)\cap S\neq\emptyset$). Then the sets $x_1^{-1}(W)\subset\Re^2$ and $x_2^{-1}(W)\subset\Re^2$ are open sets and the map:$$\Huge x_2^{-1}\circ x_1:x_1^{-1}(W)\rightarrow x_2^{-1}(W)$$is a diffeomorphism. This is similar to the [[Differentiable Manifolds#Definition|coordinate changes]] for a differentiable manifold. Proof omitted.

The unit sphere $S^2$ is a regular surface given by:$$\Huge S^2=\{(x,y,z)\in\Re^3:x^2+y^2+z^2=1\}$$which we showed can be covered by six local parametrisations, each covering an open hemisphere of $S^2$. Let us discuss another important system of two local parametrisations covering $S^2$. called stereographic projections. Let $N=(0,0,1)$ and $S=(0,0,-1)$ be the north and south poles of $S^2$ respectively. The first local parametrisation $x_1$ covers $S^3\setminus\{N\}$ and is given by:$$\Huge x_1:\Re^2\rightarrow S^2\setminus\{N\},\,\,x_1(u,v)=\frac{1}{u^2+v^2+1}(2u,2v,u^2+v^2-1)$$For $(u,v)\in\Re^2$, $x_1(u,v)$ is the unique intersection point of the straight line $l$ through $(u,v,0)$ and $N$ with $S^2\setminus\{N\}$:$$\Huge\{x_1(u,v)\}=l\cap S^2\setminus\{N\}$$Similarly, the second local parametrisation covers $S^2\setminus\{S\}$ and is defined by a similar geometric construction. Namely, $x_2(u,v)$ is the unique intersection point of the straight line through $(u,v,0)$ and $S$ with $S^2\setminus\{S\}$. It can be shown that the inverse $x_2^{-1}:S^2\setminus\{S\}\rightarrow\Re^2$ is given by:$$\Huge x_2^{-1}(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{1+z}(x,y)$$It is clear that the local parametrisation $x_1$ and $x_2$ cover all $S^2$ and they overlap in $W=x_1(\Re^2)\cap x_2(\Re^2)=S^2\setminus\{S,N\}$. Now we calculate change of parametrisation $x_2^{-1}\circ x_1:x_1^{-1}(W)\rightarrow x_2^{-1}(W)$. Firstly, it is easy to see that both parametrisations coincide with $\Re^2\setminus\{0\}$. Therefore we have:$$\Huge x_2^{-1}\circ x_1:\Re^2\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\Re^2\setminus\{0\}$$Using the above formulae yields for $(u,v)\in\Re^2\setminus\{0\}$:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
x_1(u,v)&=\frac{1}{u^2+v^2+1}(2u,2v,u^2+v^2-1)=(x,y,z)\\
1+z&=\frac{2(u^2+v^2)}{u^2+v^2+1}\\
x_2^{-1}\circ x_1(u,v)&=x_2^{-1}(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{1+z}(x,y)=\frac{1}{u^2+v^2}(u,v)
\end{align*}$$Note that this is simply $(u,v)$ for points on the unit circle. This agrees with the fact that $u^2+v^2=1$ forces both parametrisations to satisfy:$$\Huge x_1(u,v)=x_2(u,v)=(u,v,0)$$Moreover, we have that the map:$$\Huge\phi:x_2^{-1}\circ x_1:\Re^2\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\Re^2\setminus\{0\}$$is a diffeomorphism. 

# Special surfaces:

## Revolution:
A surface of revolution is obtained by rotating a curve in the $(x,z)$ coordinate plane around the $z$-axis. We start with an open interval $I\subset\Re$ and a simple smooth regular curve $\underline{\alpha}:I\rightarrow\Re^3$ of the form $\underline{\alpha}(v)=(f(v),0,g(v))$. Assume that $f(v)\neq0$ for all $v\in I$, then we rotate and obtain:$$\Huge S=\{(f(v)\cos u,f(v)\sin u,g(v)):u\in\Re,v\in I\}$$The resulting surface is a surface of revolution and $\underline{\alpha}$ is called its generating curve. The construction is illustrated as such:![[Surfaces in R3 2025-11-21 00.22.45.excalidraw]]There are two families of special curves associated with this surface. The circles $u\mapsto(f(v)\cos u,f(v)\sin u,g(v))$ are called parallels of $S$ and the curves obtained by rotating $\underline{\alpha}$ by a fixed angle $u_0\in\Re$ that is $v\mapsto(f(v)\cos u_0,f(v)\sin u_0,g(v))$ are called meridians of $S$.

## Canal:
Let $\underline{\alpha}:I\rightarrow\Re^3$ be a simple smooth unit speed curve. For every $s\in I$ we call the plane through $\underline{\alpha}(s)$ and spanned by the principle normal vector $\underline{n}_\underline{\alpha}(s)$ and binormal vector $\underline{b}_\underline{\alpha}(s)$ the normal plane at $\underline{\alpha}(s)$, denoted by $N_\underline{\alpha}(s)$. For a given $r>0$ we can then consider the family of circles in $N_\underline{\alpha}(s)$ with center $\underline{\alpha}(s)$ and radius $r$. If the curvature $\kappa:I\rightarrow\Re$ is bounded, we can choose radius $r$ small enough such that the union of these circles form a regular surface $S_{\underline{\alpha},r}\subset\Re^3$, called a canal surface. This is parametrised by:$$\Huge x(s,\theta)=\underline{\alpha}(s)+r\cdot(\cos(\theta)\underline{n}(s)+\sin(\theta)\underline{b}(s))$$![[Surfaces in R3 2025-11-21 14.07.56.excalidraw]]
## Ruled:
Let $\underline{\alpha}:I\rightarrow\Re^3$ be a simple smooth regular curve and $\underline{w}:I\rightarrow\Re^3$ be a smooth map which is never zero. Moreover, assume that $\underline{\alpha}'(u)$ and $\underline{w}(u)$ are never parallel. We consider the family of straight line segments through $\underline{\alpha}(u)$ and parallel to $\underline{w}(u)$, that is we choose an open interval $J=(-a,a)$ with $a>0$ and introduce the following global parametrisation $x:I\times J\rightarrow\Re^3$:$$\Huge x(u,v)=\underline{\alpha}(u)+v\underline{w}(u)$$If the image of this map is a surface, we call it a ruled surface. The simplest examples of ruled surfaces are affine planes in $\Re^3$.![[Surfaces in R3 2025-11-21 14.20.23.excalidraw]]

## Example:
Recall the one-sheeted hyperboloid that was given as a level set of the function $f:\Re^3\rightarrow\Re$, $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-z^2$:$$\Huge S=f^{-1}(1)=\{(x,y,z)\in\Re^3:x^2+y^2-z^2=1\}$$The regular surface $S$ is both a surface of revolution and a ruled surface:
> Intersecting $S$ with $y=0$ yields the set $\{(x,0,z):x^2-z^2=1\}$. This can be viewed as the union of two disjoint curves given by $(\pm\cosh v,0,\sinh v)$. Choosing one of these curves, we have a simple smooth regular curve in the $(x,z)$-coordinate plane that does not meet the $z$-axis. Rotation yields:$$\Huge S'=\{(\cosh(v)\cos(u),\cosh(v)\sin(u),\sinh(v)):u,v\in\Re\}$$We claim that $S'$ agrees with $S$, showing that $S$ is a surface of revolution. 
> Firstly we show $S'\subset S$. Let $x=\cosh(v)\cos(u),y=\cosh(v)\sin(u)$ and $z=\sinh(v)$:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
f(x,y,z)&=(\cosh(v)\cos(u))^2+(\cosh(v)\sin(u))^2-\sinh^2(v)\\
&=\cosh^2(v)-\sinh^2(v)=1
\end{align*}$$which shows that $S'\subset f^{-1}(1)$. 
> It remains to show that $S\subset S'$. Let $(x,y,z)\in S$, that is $x^2+y^2-z^2=1$, then we have $x^2+y^2\geq1$ and we can find a unique $v\in[0,\infty)$ with $\cosh^2v=x^2+y^2$. Since $\cosh^2v-\sinh^2v=1$ we see that $\sinh^2v=z^2$ and, therefore $z=\sinh v$ or $z=-\sinh v=\sinh(-v)$. Since $\cosh(v)=\cosh(-v)$ we can find $v\in\Re$ with:$$\Huge x^2+y^2=\cosh^2v,\,\,z=\sinh v$$This means that $(x,y)$ lies on the circle of radius $\cosh v$ around the origin and we can find $u\in[0,2\pi)\subset\Re$ such that:$$\Huge (x,y)=(\cosh(v)\cos(u),\cosh(v)\sin(u))$$Therefore we have:$$\Huge (x,y,z)=(\cosh(v)\cos(u),\cosh(v)\sin(u),\sinh(v))\in S'$$completing the proof that $S\subset S'$.
> Intersecting $S$ with the $(x,y)$-coordinate plane yields a unit circle:$$\Huge S\cap\{(x,y,0):x,y\in\Re\}=\{(x,y,0)\in\Re^3:x^2+y^2=1\}$$We will see that $S$ can be viewed as a ruled surface over the curve $\underline{\alpha}(u)=(\cos u,\sin u,0)$, parametrising this unit circle. We need to find $\underline{w}(u)$ such that:$$\Huge x(u,v)=\underline{\alpha}(u)+v\underline{w}(u)$$parametrises the surface $S$. We set $\underline{w}(u)=(w_1(u),w_2(u),w_3(u))$, then the condition:$$\Huge x(u,v)=(\cos u+vw_1(u),\sin u+vw_2(u),vw_3(u))\in S$$for all $u,v$ is equivalent to:$$\Huge (\cos u+vw_1(u))^2+(\sin u+vw_2(u))^2-(vw_3(u))^2=1$$for all $u,v$. This is a polynomial in $v$, given by:$$\Huge 2(w_1(u)\cos u+w_2(u)\sin u)v+(w_1^2(u)+w_2^2(u)-w_3^2(u))v^2=0$$Since this must hold for any choice of $v$, we conclude that both terms in the polynomial must be $0$ for all $u$. These are simultaneously satisfied by:$$\Huge w_1(u)=\sin u,\,\,w_2(u)=-\cos u,\,\,w_3(u)=\pm 1$$Then both choices:$$\Huge \underline{w}(u)=(\sin u,-\cos u,1),\,\,\underline{w}=(\sin u,-\cos u,-1)$$lead to a ruled surface. That is, $S$ can be thought of as a doubly ruled surface.
