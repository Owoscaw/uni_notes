
We investigate the geometric properties of [[Curves and Surfaces]] in $\Re^3$ Euclidean space. Firstly we define local, analytic properties and build global results that link geometry to [[Topological spaces#Topologies|topology]]:
> We define regular curves as smooth maps into $\Re^n$ where the tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'=0$.
> Using the tangent vector we define the unit tangent and [[Tangent planes#Lengths, angles and areas|arc length]], allowing reparametrisation of curves to unit speed.
> We also define a quantitative measure of how much the curve "curves":
> > For [[Curves and Surfaces#Curves and curvature|plane curves]] we define the curvature $\kappa$ as how the unit tangent vector changes direction.
> > For [[Space curves|space curves]] there exists another invariant called torsion $\tau$, defined to measure the curve's "twist". These are defined through the [[Space curves#Serret-Frenet equations|Serret-Frenet equations]].
> A regular surface is a subset of $\Re^3$ that we cover with a smooth [[Surfaces in R3#Parametrisations of regular surfaces|local parametrisation]]. At every point $\underline{p}$ we define a tangent plane spanned by the partial derivatives of the parametrisation.
> We also define the plane's [[Tangent planes#First fundamental form|first fundamental form]] as the inner product of tangent vectors. This is the metric of the surface and allows for the calculation of other intrinsic properties like curve lengths, angles, and areas.
> To further understand surfaces, we define the [[Geometry of the Gauss map|Gauss map]] that assigns a unit normal vector to every point. From this, we define the [[Geometry of the Gauss map#Weingarten map and second fundamental form|Weingarten map]] as the negative derivative of the Gauss map. 
> From the Weingarten map, we derive the essential [[Geometry of the Gauss map#Curvatures|curvatures]]:
> > Principal curvatures $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$ are the eigenvalues of the Weingarten map.
> > Gaussian curvature $K$ is the determinant $K=\kappa_1\kappa_2$.
> > Mean curvature $H$ is half of the trace $H=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)$.
> Now we bridge the gap between local measurements and global structure.
> Gauss' [[Theorema Egregium of Gauss]] proves that Gaussian curvature is intrinsic, depending only on the first fundamental form and not the space the surface is embedded into (usually $\Re^3$).
