
# The tangent plane:

We introduce tangent planes of a regular surface $S\subset\Re^3$ as the plane a consisting of all tangent vectors of smooth curves in the surface passing through some $\underline{p}\in S$. That is, the tangent plane at $\underline{p}$ is the best linear approximation of the surface $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

## Definition:
Let $S\subset\Re^3$ be a [[Surfaces in R3|regular surface]]. A tangent vector to $S$ at $\underline{p}\in S$ is a tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)$ of a smooth curve $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ with $\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}$.

We introduce the tangent plane at $\underline{p}\in S$ using a local parametrisation. Let $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ be a local parametrisation with $\underline{q}\in U$ and with $\underline{x}(\underline{q})=\underline{p}$. Then the image $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset\Re^3$ is a linear subspace of $\Re^3$ generated by $\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(\underline{q}),\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(\underline{q})\in\Re^3$. For simplicity, we introduce:$$\Huge \underline{x}_u(\underline{q})=\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(\underline{q}),\,\,\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})=\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(\underline{q})$$By construction these are linearly independent ($\dim(d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2))=2$). It turns out that this $2$-dimensional subspace coincides the set of all tangent vectors to $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

Let $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ be a local parametrisation of a surface $S$ with $\underline{q}\in U$ and $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$. Then $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset\Re^3$ is a $2$-dimensional subspace and coincides with all tangent vectors to $S$ at $\underline{p}$. The plane $T_\underline{p}S=d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)$ is called the tangent plane to $S$ at $\underline{p}$. Moreover this plane does not depend on the choice of local parametrisation. Proof:
> Let $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$. We will prove the above using a proposition by showing:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)=\{\underline{\alpha}'(0):\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S\text{ smooth },\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}\}=A$$
> Since the set $A$ is not related to any particular local parametrisation, this identity shows that the tangent space at $\underline{p}$ does not depend on the choice of parametrisation. We show this equality by two inclusions.
> We proceed to show that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\supset A$. Let $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ be a smooth curve with $\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}$. We can assume WLOG that $\underline{\alpha}(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\subset \underline{x}(U)$. Consider the map $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U,\underline{\beta}(t)=\underline{x}^{-1}\circ\underline{\alpha}(t)$. It can be shown that $\underline{\beta}$ is smooth and it is clear that $\underline{\beta}(0)=\underline{x}^{-1}(\underline{p})=\underline{q}$. Using the chain rule we obtain:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)=\frac{d}{dt}(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})(t)|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{\alpha}(t)|_{t=0}=\underline{\alpha}'(0)$$showing that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\supset A$.
> Now we show the converse, $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset A$. Let $\underline{v}\in\Re^2$ and choose some $\epsilon>0$ such that $\underline{\beta}(t)=\underline{q}+t\underline{v}\in U$ for all $t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)$. Then using the chain rule we obtain:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\underline{v})=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}(\underline{q}+t\underline{v})|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}(t)|_{t=0}=(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)$$The curve $\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ is smooth with $(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})(0)=\underline{x}(\underline{q})=\underline{p}$, showing that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset A$.

We saw in this proof that a local parametrisation $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ can be used to recover a curve $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow\underline{x}(U)\subset S$ to a curve $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U\subset\Re^2$. This process also goes the other way round, that is given a curve $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U\subset\Re^2$ in the domain of a local parametrisation $\underline{x}$ a second curve is induced $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ in the surface via the composition $\underline{\alpha}=\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}$. This curve $\underline{\alpha}$ defines a tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)\in T_\underline{p}S$ with $\underline{p}=\underline{\alpha}(0)\in S$. We know that the tangent plane $T_\underline{p}S$ is spanned by the vectors $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$ where $\underline{q}=\underline{x}^{-1}(\underline{p})$. Therefore we can write:$$\Huge \underline{\alpha}'(0)=(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)=a\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+b\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$$with suitable $a,b$. We can find these coefficients by introducing the component functions of the curve $\underline{\beta}$, that is we write $\underline{\beta}=(u(t),v(t))$:$$\Huge \implies(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}(u(t),v(t))|_{t=0}=u'(0)\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+v'(0)\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$$We conclude the fact that the tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)$ has coordinates $(u'(0),v'(0))$ with respect to the basis $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\in T_\underline{q}(S)$.

## Example:
Let $U\subset\Re^2$ be an open set and $g:U\rightarrow\Re$ be a smooth function. We know that the graph of $g$ given by:$$\Huge S=\{(u,v,g(u,v)):(u,v)\in U\}\subset\Re^3$$is a regular surface with global parametrisation $\underline{x}(u,v)=(u,v,g(u,v))$. Then the tangent plane $T_\underline{p}S$ to $S$ at $\underline{p}=(u,v,g(u,v))$ is generated by:$$\Huge \underline{x}_u(u,v)=\left(1,0,\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}(u,v)\right),\,\,\underline{x}_v(u,v)=\left(0,1,\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}(u,v)\right)$$
Regular surfaces are often given as level sets of a [[Surfaces in R3#Regular points/values|regular value]] $c\in f(U)$ of a smooth function $f:U\rightarrow\Re,U\subset\Re^3$ open . The next lemme provides a geometric description of the tangent plane of the regular surface $S=f^{-1}(c)\subset\Re^3$:
> Let $U\subset\Re^3$ be open, $f:U\rightarrow\Re$ be a smooth map and $c\in f(U)$ be a regular value of $f$. Let $\underline{p}\in S=f^{-1}(c)$. Then the tangent plane $T_\underline{p}S$ is the Euclidean plane which is orthogonal to $\underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})$.
> To prove this, consider that any tangent vector in $T_\underline{p}S$ can be described by a smooth curve $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ with $\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}$. Since $S=f|^{-1}(c)$ we have:$$\Huge f\circ\underline{\alpha}(t)=c\,\,\forall t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)$$Differentiation yields $(f\circ\underline{\alpha})'(0)=0$, which can be rewritten with the chain rule as:$$\Huge 0=(f\circ\underline{\alpha})'(0)=d_{\underline{\alpha}(0)}f(\underline{\alpha}'(0))=\underline{\nabla}f(\underline{\alpha}(0))\cdot\underline{\alpha}'(0)=0$$showing that $\underline{\alpha}'(0)$ is perpendicular to $\underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})$. Since $\underline{\nabla}f(\underline{p})\neq0$, its orthogonal complement is a $2$-dimensional subspace of $\Re^3$ containing $T_\underline{p}S$. On the other hand, $T_\underline{p}S$ is also $2$-dimensional and therefore both subspaces must coincide.

# First fundamental form:

In order to define the lengths of curves, angles between tangent vectors, and areas of domains in a surface $S$, we now introduce natural bilinear forms on the tangent planes. These bilinear forms are derived from the canonical inner product in Euclidean $3$-space $\Re^3$, namely the map $\Re^3\times\Re^3\rightarrow\Re,(\underline{v},\underline{w})\rightarrow\underline{v}\cdot\underline{w}=\sum_{i=1}^3v_iw_i$, where $\underline{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ and $\underline{w}=(w_1,w_2,w_3)$. Since the tangent space $T_\underline{p}S$ at $\underline{p}\in S$ is a $2$-dimensional subspace of $\Re^3$, we can restrict the canonical inner product of $\Re^3$ to $T_\underline{p}S$. We denote this restriction by $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_\underline{p}$, that is:$$\Huge \langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_\underline{p}:T_\underline{p}S\times T_\underline{p}S\rightarrow\Re,\,\,(\underline{w}_1,\underline{w}_2)=\underline{w}_1\cdot\underline{w}_2$$which has the properties:
> Bilinearity, $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_\underline{p}$ is linear in both arguments
> Symmetry, $\langle \underline{w}_1,\underline{w}_2\rangle_\underline{p}=\langle \underline{w}_2,\underline{w}_1\rangle_\underline{p}$
> Positivity, $||\underline{w}||_\underline{p}^2=\langle \underline{w},\underline{w}\rangle\geq0$ for all $\underline{w}\in T_\underline{p}S$ and $||\underline{w}||_\underline{p}^2=0$ implies $\underline{w}=0\in T_\underline{p}S$

This bilinear form on the tangent plane allows us to define:
> The length of a tangent vector $\underline{w}\in T_\underline{p}S$ by:$$\Huge ||\underline{w}||_\underline{p}=\sqrt{\langle \underline{w},\underline{w}\rangle_\underline{p}}$$
> The angle between two tangent vectors $\underline{w}_1,\underline{w}_2\in T_\underline{p}S$ by:$$\Huge \cos\theta=\frac{\langle \underline{w}_1,\underline{w}_2\rangle_\underline{p}}{||\underline{w}_1||_\underline{p}\cdot||\underline{w}_2||_\underline{p}}$$

Note that the bilinear form induces a quadratic form $\underline{I}_\underline{p}:T\underline{p}M\rightarrow\Re$, given by $\underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w})=\langle \underline{w},\underline{w}\rangle_\underline{p}$. Moreover, the bilinear form and the quadratic form mutually determine each other. We recover the bilinear form from its quadratic form using the parallelogram equation:$$\Huge \langle \underline{w}_1,\underline{w}_2\rangle\underline{p}=\frac{1}{2}(\underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w}_1+\underline{w}_1)-\underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w}_1)-\underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w}_2))$$These inner products and their quadratic forms are called the first fundamental form of the surface and they are key to geometry on regular surfaces.

Let $S\subset\Re^3$ be a regular surface and $\underline{p}\in S$. The quadratic form $\underline{I}_\underline{p}:T_\underline{p}S\rightarrow\Re$$$\Huge \underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w})=\langle \underline{w},\underline{w}\rangle_\underline{p}=||\underline{w}||_\underline{p}^2$$is called the first fundamental form or metric of $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

While it is natural for a regular surface $S\subset\Re^2$ to choose the restriction of the canonical inner product of the ambient Euclidean $3$-space for the quadratic form, we could also allow for others on the tangent spaces of $S$. This additional flexibility allows us to investigate the geometry of more general $2$-dimensional surfaces $S$, which may no longer be given as regular surfaces in ambient Euclidean $3$-space. 

Our next aim is to express the first fundamental form $\underline{I}_\underline{p}$ in terms of a local parametrisation. Let $U\subset\Re^2$ be open and $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ be a local parametrisation of $S$ at $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$. We have seen that the vectors $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$ form a basis of the tangent plane $T_\underline{p}S$ and we can write any tangent vector as a linear combination of these two vectors. We can therefore write the first fundamental form as:$$\Huge \begin{align*}
\underline{I}_\underline{p}&=\langle \underline{w},\underline{w}\rangle_\underline{p}\\
&=\langle a\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+b\underline{x}_v(\underline{q)},a\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+b\underline{x}_v(\underline{q)}\rangle_\underline{p}\\
&=a^2 \langle \underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})\rangle_\underline{p}+2ab \langle \underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\rangle_\underline{p}+b^2\langle \underline{x}_v(\underline{q}_,\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\rangle
\end{align*}$$Let $U\subset\Re^2$ be open and $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S\subset\Re^3$ be a local parametrisation of a regular surface at $S$. The functions $E,F,G:U\rightarrow\Re$ given by:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
E(\underline{q})&=\langle \underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})\rangle_{\underline{x}(\underline{q})}\\
F(\underline{q})&=\langle \underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\rangle_{\underline{x}(\underline{q})}\\
G(\underline{q})&=\langle \underline{x}_v(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\rangle_{\underline{x}(\underline{q})}
\end{align*}$$are called the coefficients of the first fundamental form with respect to $\underline{x}$.

Note that if $(a,b)\in\Re^2$ are coefficients of a vector $\underline{w}\in T_\underline{p}S$ with respect to the basis $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$ with $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$ then:$$\Huge \begin{align*}
\underline{I}_\underline{p}(\underline{w})&=a^2E(\underline{q})+2abF(\underline{q})+b^2G(\underline{q})\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}a & b\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}E(\underline{q}) & F(\underline{q})\\
F(\underline{q}) & G(\underline{q})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}$$Since $\underline{I}_\underline{p}$ is positive, we have:$$\Huge E>0,\,\,G>0,\,\,\det\begin{pmatrix}E & F \\ F & G\end{pmatrix}=EG-F^2>0$$Moreover, $F=0$ means that the tangent vectors $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\in T_{\underline{x}(\underline{q})}S$ are orthogonal for every $\underline{q}\in U$. It can be shown that for any regular surface $S\subset\Re^3$ and $\underline{p}\in S$, there exists a local parametrisation $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ of $S$ at $\underline{p}$ such that its coefficients of the first fundamental form satisfy $F=0$ and $E=G$. This means that the tangent vectors are orthogonal and have the same length. We call such a local parametrisation an isothermal parametrisation.

## Examples:
> Let us compute the coefficients of the first fundamental form for the graph of $f:\Re^2\rightarrow\Re$ given by $f(u,v)=u^2-v^2$. This is a hyperbolic paraboloid. We have one global parametrisation, given by $\underline{x}(u,v)=(u,v,u^2-v^2)$, and:$$\Huge \underline{x}_u(u,v)=(1,0,2u),\,\,\underline{x}_v(u,v)=(0,1,-2v)$$This implies that:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
E(u,v)&=(1,0,2u)\cdot(1,0,2u)=1+4u^2\\
F(u,v)&=(1,0,2u)\cdot(0,1,-2v)=-4uv\\
G(u,v)&=(0,1,-2v)\cdot(0,1,-2v)=1+4v^2
\end{align*}$$In particular at $(u,v)=(0,0)$ we have:$$\Huge\begin{pmatrix}E(0,0) & F(0,0) \\ F(0,0) & G(0,0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$Meaning that the tangent vectors form an orthonormal basis of $T_{(0,0)}S$.
> Let $S\subset\Re^3$ be a surface of revolution, that is we can choose parametrisations of the form:$$\Huge \underline{x}(u,v)=(f(v)\cos u,f(v)\sin u,g(v))$$we obtain:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
\underline{x}_u(u,v)&=(-f(v)\sin u,f(v)\cos u,0)\\
\underline{x}_v(u,v)&=(f'(v)\cos u,f'(v)\sin u,g'(v))
\end{align*}$$and therefore we obtain:$$\Huge\begin{align*}
E(u,v)&=f(v)^2\\
F(u,v)&=0\\
G(u,v)&=||\underline{\alpha}(v)||^2
\end{align*}$$where $\underline{\alpha}(v)=(f(v),0,g(v))$ is the generating curve of $S$.
