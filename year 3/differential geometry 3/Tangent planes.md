
# The tangent plane:

We introduce tangent planes of a regular surface $S\subset\Re^3$ as the plane a consisting of all tangent vectors of smooth curves in the surface passing through some $\underline{p}\in S$. That is, the tangent plane at $\underline{p}$ is the best linear approximation of the surface $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

## Definition:
Let $S\subset\Re^3$ be a [[Surfaces in R3|regular surface]]. A tangent vector to $S$ at $\underline{p}\in S$ is a tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)$ of a smooth curve $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ with $\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}$.

We introduce the tangent plane at $\underline{p}\in S$ using a local parametrisation. Let $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ be a local parametrisation with $\underline{q}\in U$ and with $\underline{x}(\underline{q})=\underline{p}$. Then the image $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset\Re^3$ is a linear subspace of $\Re^3$ generated by $\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(\underline{q}),\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(\underline{q})\in\Re^3$. For simplicity, we introduce:$$\Huge \underline{x}_u(\underline{q})=\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial u}(\underline{q}),\,\,\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})=\frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial v}(\underline{q})$$By construction these are linearly independent ($\dim(d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2))=2$). It turns out that this $2$-dimensional subspace coincides the set of all tangent vectors to $S$ at $\underline{p}$.

Let $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ be a local parametrisation of a surface $S$ with $\underline{q}\in U$ and $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$. Then $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset\Re^3$ is a $2$-dimensional subspace and coincides with all tangent vectors to $S$ at $\underline{p}$. The plane $T_\underline{p}S=d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)$ is called the tangent plane to $S$ at $\underline{p}$. Moreover this plane does not depend on the choice of local parametrisation. Proof:
> Let $\underline{p}=\underline{x}(\underline{q})$. We will prove the above using a proposition by showing:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)=\{\underline{\alpha}'(0):\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S\text{ smooth },\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}\}=A$$
> Since the set $A$ is not related to any particular local parametrisation, this identity shows that the tangent space at $\underline{p}$ does not depend on the choice of parametrisation. We show this equality by two inclusions.
> We proceed to show that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\supset A$. Let $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ be a smooth curve with $\underline{\alpha}(0)=\underline{p}$. We can assume WLOG that $\underline{\alpha}(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\subset \underline{x}(U)$. Consider the map $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U,\underline{\beta}(t)=\underline{x}^{-1}\circ\underline{\alpha}(t)$. It can be shown that $\underline{\beta}$ is smooth and it is clear that $\underline{\beta}(0)=\underline{x}^{-1}(\underline{p})=\underline{q}$. Using the chain rule we obtain:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)=\frac{d}{dt}(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})(t)|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{\alpha}(t)|_{t=0}=\underline{\alpha}'(0)$$showing that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\supset A$.
> Now we show the converse, $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset A$. Let $\underline{v}\in\Re^2$ and choose some $\epsilon>0$ such that $\underline{\beta}(t)=\underline{q}+t\underline{v}\in U$ for all $t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)$. Then using the chain rule we obtain:$$\Huge d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\underline{v})=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}(\underline{q}+t\underline{v})|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}(t)|_{t=0}=(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)$$The curve $\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ is smooth with $(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})(0)=\underline{x}(\underline{q})=\underline{p}$, showing that $d_\underline{q}\underline{x}(\Re^2)\subset A$.

We saw in this proof that a local parametrisation $\underline{x}:U\rightarrow S$ can be used to recover a curve $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow\underline{x}(U)\subset S$ to a curve $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U\subset\Re^2$. This process also goes the other way round, that is given a curve $\underline{\beta}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow U\subset\Re^2$ in the domain of a local parametrisation $\underline{x}$ a second curve is induced $\underline{\alpha}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow S$ in the surface via the composition $\underline{\alpha}=\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta}$. This curve $\underline{\alpha}$ defines a tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)\in T_\underline{p}S$ with $\underline{p}=\underline{\alpha}(0)\in S$. We know that the tangent plane $T_\underline{p}S$ is spanned by the vectors $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$ where $\underline{q}=\underline{x}^{-1}(\underline{p})$. Therefore we can write:$$\Huge \underline{\alpha}'(0)=(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)=a\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+b\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$$with suitable $a,b$. We can find these coefficients by introducing the component functions of the curve $\underline{\beta}$, that is we write $\underline{\beta}=(u(t),v(t))$:$$\Huge \implies(\underline{x}\circ\underline{\beta})'(0)=\frac{d}{dt}\underline{x}(u(t),v(t))|_{t=0}=u'(0)\underline{x}_u(\underline{q})+v'(0)\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})$$We conclude the fact that the tangent vector $\underline{\alpha}'(0)$ has coordinates $(u'(0),v'(0))$ with respect to the basis $\underline{x}_u(\underline{q}),\underline{x}_v(\underline{q})\in T_\underline{q}(S)$.
